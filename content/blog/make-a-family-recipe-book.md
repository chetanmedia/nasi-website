To make a family recipe book, gather every recipe you can find, choose the ones that matter most, test and standardize them, add the stories and photos behind them, then put it all together as a digital collection, a printed book or both. The hard part is not the design. It is getting the recipes out of drawers, phones and people's heads before they are lost. This guide walks through each step, with tips for doing it as a family project.

## Why make a family recipe book at all?

Family recipes are fragile. They live on one stained index card, in one relative's memory, or in a video someone sent years ago. When that card gets lost or that person is no longer around to ask, the dish can disappear with them.

A family recipe book fixes that. It gives everyone the same, tested version of each dish. It also captures the stories: who made the pie every year, why the stuffing has raisins, what "a handful" actually meant. That is the part people treasure most.

### It makes a lasting gift

A printed family cookbook is a popular gift for weddings, new homes, graduations and milestone birthdays. A shared digital one means a cousin across the country can make the same lasagna on the same night.

## How do I collect recipes from the whole family?

Start wide, then narrow down. You want every candidate in one place before you decide what makes the cut.

### Ask early and make it easy

Send a short message to relatives explaining the project and asking for 2 or 3 recipes each. Give a deadline. Make it easy to contribute in whatever form they have:

- A photo of a recipe card or cookbook page
- A typed recipe in a text or email
- A link to a cooking video they always use
- A voice memo where they talk through the dish

Some relatives will send polished recipes. Others will send "you just know when it looks right." Both are useful.

### Go hunting in the house

Check recipe boxes, the margins of old cookbooks, church and community cookbooks, envelopes tucked into drawers, and the backs of photos. Handwritten notes on a printed recipe are often the real family version.

### Interview the cooks

For dishes nobody ever wrote down, cook alongside the person who makes them. Measure what they add before it goes in the pot. Write down times, pan sizes and how they judge doneness. Ask them questions as you go, and record the answers if they are happy for you to.

### Scan cards before anything else

Photograph or scan each handwritten card in even daylight, front and back. Do it before the cards get handled again. Store the originals in acid-free sleeves, away from heat and sunlight.

## How do I choose and test the recipes?

Once you have a pile, it is time to edit.

### Pick your list

Sort recipes into rough groups: the must-haves everyone asks for, the holiday dishes, the everyday favorites, and the "nice to have" ones. For a first edition, 20 to 40 recipes is a manageable size. If two relatives have different versions of the same dish, consider including both with each person's name. That is part of the family story too.

### Test and standardize

Cook each recipe at least once from the written version, exactly as written. You will find gaps fast. Fix them as you go:

- Turn vague amounts ("a glass of milk," "butter the size of an egg") into standard measurements.
- Add pan sizes, oven temperatures and approximate times.
- List ingredients in the order they are used.
- Write steps as short, numbered actions.
- Note servings, prep time and cook time.

If an old recipe calls for something hard to find, add a swap. Our [ingredient substitution chart](../ingredient-substitution-chart/) covers the common ones, and the [substitute finder](../../tools/substitute-finder/) can help with the rest.

### Use one recipe template

Consistency makes a book easy to cook from. A simple template works well:

1. Recipe name
2. Who it came from, and a sentence or two about it
3. Servings, prep time and cook time
4. Ingredients with amounts
5. Numbered steps
6. Notes, tips and swaps

## What should go in a family recipe book besides recipes?

The extras are what turn a recipe collection into a family heirloom.

### Stories and memories

Add a short note to each recipe. When was it made? Who always asked for seconds? What went wrong the first time you tried it? A few sentences are enough.

### Photos

Include photos of the finished dish, the cooks, family meals over the years, and scans of the original handwritten cards. A card in someone's handwriting, reproduced in the book, can mean more than any polished food photo.

### Helpful pages

Consider adding a contents page, an index by ingredient, a family tree showing who contributed what, a holiday menu or two, and a simple measurement conversion page.

## How do I make a digital family recipe book?

A digital family cookbook is the fastest to build, the easiest to update and the simplest to share. There are a few common routes.

### A shared document or folder

A shared document or a folder of files is free and familiar. It works well for small collections. The downside is that recipes in a long document are hard to search while cooking, and formatting can drift as different people edit.

### A design tool or slide template

Design tools and recipe card templates let you create pretty pages, which is handy if you plan to print later. They take more time per recipe, and they are better for presentation than for everyday cooking.

### A recipe app

A recipe app keeps every recipe in the same clean format, makes them searchable, and lets you cook from your phone. This is the most practical option if you want the family to actually use the recipes week to week, and you can still print from it later.

### Digital vs printed at a glance

| | Digital | Printed |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Often free or low cost | Printing cost per copy |
| Easy to update | Yes, anytime | Needs a new print run |
| Searchable | Yes | Index only |
| Sharing | Instant, to anyone | Mail or hand over copies |
| Keepsake value | Lower | High |
| Best for | Everyday cooking | Gifts and heirlooms |

## How do I make a printed family recipe book?

Once your recipes are tested and formatted, printing is mostly a design and production job.

### Choose a format

- **Binder:** Print pages at home and put them in sheet protectors. Cheap, spill-friendly and easy to add to.
- **Spiral bound:** A copy shop can print and bind your pages. It lies flat on the counter while you cook.
- **Photo book:** Online photo book services produce a polished hardcover or softcover. Great for gifts, and you can order as many copies as you need.

### Design tips

- Keep each recipe on one page or one two-page spread, so nobody has to flip mid-step.
- Use a large, readable font for ingredients and steps.
- Leave white space and room for handwritten notes.
- Group recipes into chapters, such as breakfast, mains, sides, desserts and holidays.
- Proofread every amount and temperature twice. Have a second person check too.

### Plan your timeline

If the book is a holiday gift, work backward. Leave several weeks for collecting, a few more for testing and writing, and allow plenty of time for printing and shipping, which can slow down a lot in December.

## How can I make it a family project?

The best family cookbooks are built together.

- Assign each branch of the family a chapter.
- Host a cooking day where relatives make their dish while someone takes notes and photos.
- Ask kids to draw pictures or write about their favorite family meal.
- Let older relatives tell the stories, even if someone else writes them down.
