The best free recipe organizer app is the one whose free plan fits how you actually save recipes. If your recipes start as TikTok, Instagram Reels or Facebook videos, choose a free app that turns a video into a written recipe. If you mostly clip from recipe websites, a free web clipper like Copy Me That or the free tier of Recipe Keeper will serve you better. Below we lay six free tiers side by side, explain the limits that matter, and help you pick one in five minutes.

## What makes a free recipe organizer app worth using?

A recipe organizer has one job: get a recipe out of wherever you found it and into a place where you can find it and cook from it later. Free plans differ a lot in how well they do that job.

### The four things we checked

- **Where it can import from.** Social video, recipe websites, photos, PDFs or typed text.
- **What the free limit is.** A cap on recipes, a cap on imports per week, or missing features.
- **How it helps you cook.** Search, servings scaling, shopping lists, meal plans and a cook mode.
- **Where it runs.** iPhone only, or phone plus web and desktop.

### A note on prices and limits

Free tiers change often. We only list a number where we could confirm it on the app's own site or store listing. Where we could not, we say "check the app." Always look at the paywall screen before you rely on a free plan.

## What does each free plan actually give you?

### Nasi: free for recipes that start as videos

Nasi was built for the recipes you find while scrolling. Share a TikTok, Instagram Reel or Facebook video to Nasi, or paste the link. It watches the video, listens to the creator and reads the caption, then writes the ingredients with amounts, numbered steps, servings and cook time, with a link back to the creator.

The free plan gives you 3 imports every week. You can also scan a photo, screenshot, cookbook page or handwritten card, upload a PDF (one recipe per PDF), paste text, or type a recipe by hand. Nasi does not open recipe website, YouTube or Pinterest links. For those, screenshot the recipe and scan it, or copy the text and paste it in.

**Good for:** people whose "saved" folders are full of reels.
**Watch for:** iPhone only, and 3 imports a week on the free plan.

### Copy Me That: free for web clipping

Copy Me That has been around for years and does one thing well. You find a recipe on a website, tap the clipper, and it pulls the recipe into your account. The free tier covers the clipper and your recipe box, and Premium is $12.99 a year if you want extras.

**Good for:** people who save mostly from recipe blogs and want a simple, low-cost home for them.
**Watch for:** it is not built for social video.

### Recipe Keeper: free to start, one-time upgrade

Recipe Keeper feels like a classic recipe box. You can import from websites, type recipes in, and sort them into categories. The free version limits how many recipes you can store, and a one-time Pro upgrade removes the cap and adds sync. Check the app for the current limit in your region.

**Good for:** building a long-term family collection across devices.
**Watch for:** the free cap fills up faster than you expect.

### Samsung Food: free planning, paid extras

Samsung Food (the app that grew out of Whisk) is free to use for saving recipes from websites, building meal plans and making shopping lists. A paid tier adds extra planning and nutrition features.

**Good for:** people who want free meal planning more than a private archive.
**Watch for:** it leans toward discovery, so your own collection can feel secondary.

### ReciMe: social and web in one app

ReciMe saves recipes from social platforms and from websites. It has a free tier and a paid subscription. Free-tier limits and prices have changed over time, so open the paywall in the app to see what applies to you today.

**Good for:** people who want one app for both reels and blogs.
**Watch for:** check the current free limit before you commit a big collection.

### Mealime: free meal plans, not an archive

Mealime is less of an organizer and more of a planner. You pick meals from its own library and it builds a plan and grocery list. The free plan is useful. The paid tier adds more recipes and features.

**Good for:** busy weeknights when you do not want to decide.
**Watch for:** it is not designed to store recipes you found elsewhere.

## Which free recipe organizer should you pick?

Start with one question: where did the last ten recipes you saved come from?

### If most came from TikTok, Instagram or Facebook

Pick an app that reads video. Screenshots of a reel rarely capture the full ingredient list, and captions often skip amounts. Nasi turns the whole video into a written recipe, and 3 free imports a week covers most people who cook from a few new recipes a week. If you also want to read more about this, see [how to save recipes from TikTok, Instagram and Facebook](../how-to-save-recipes-from-tiktok-instagram-facebook/).

### If most came from recipe websites

A web clipper is your friend. Copy Me That and Recipe Keeper both handle website recipes well on their free tiers, and both cut out the long story above the recipe card.

### If you mostly need help planning the week

Samsung Food or Mealime will get you further for free than a pure organizer. You can pair either one with a separate app for your own saved recipes. Our [guide to meal planning for a week](../how-to-meal-plan-for-a-week/) walks through a simple routine that works with any of them.

### If you have a box of handwritten cards

Choose an app with a good photo scan. Nasi, Recipe Keeper and several others can turn a photo of a card into text. If you want a printed keepsake too, see [how to make a family recipe book](../make-a-family-recipe-book/).

## What are the hidden limits of free recipe apps?

Free plans are generous at first. The friction tends to show up a few weeks in.

### Caps on recipes or imports

Some apps cap how many recipes you can store. Others, like Nasi, cap how many you can import each week but let you keep everything you have saved. A weekly import cap is easier to live with if you add a few recipes at a time. A total recipe cap hurts more once your collection grows.

### Features held back for paid users

Sync across devices, meal planning, cook mode and export are common paid features. Decide which ones you need. If you only cook on one phone, you may never miss sync.

### Export and lock-in

Before you move hundreds of recipes into any app, check whether you can get them back out. Look for an export option in settings or the help page. If there is none on the free plan, keep that in mind.

### Ads and upsells

Some free apps show ads or regular upgrade prompts. That is fair, since someone has to pay for the servers, but it changes how the app feels in the kitchen.

## How do you set up a free recipe organizer so you actually use it?

The app matters less than the habit. These steps work with any organizer.

### Start with your ten most-cooked recipes

Do not try to import everything on day one. Add the ten recipes you already cook most. You will learn the app fast and have something useful right away.

### Use a few simple cookbooks

Five or six collections are plenty: weeknight dinners, soups, baking, holidays, and "want to try." Too many folders and you stop sorting.

### Save new recipes the moment you find them

When a reel catches your eye, save it to the app right then, not to your social app's bookmarks. Bookmarks disappear into a feed. A recipe app keeps the ingredients and steps in one place. Our post on [how to save recipes on TikTok](../how-to-save-recipes-on-tiktok/) shows the share-sheet flow step by step.

### Cook from the app at least once a week

The first time you cook from your organizer, you will notice what is missing: a step that needs clarifying, a note about oven temperature. Fix it then. A recipe you have cooked and edited is worth ten you saved and forgot.

## Is a paid recipe organizer ever worth it?

Sometimes. Pay when the free plan gets in your way every week, not before.

### Signs you have outgrown the free plan

- You hit the import or recipe cap most weeks.
- You want the same recipes on a phone and a tablet.
- You cook from the app often enough that cook mode or meal planning would save real time.

### Compare the yearly cost, not the monthly one

Many apps quote a low monthly price that adds up. Look at the yearly number and compare it to what you would spend on a single cookbook. If the app replaces a drawer of printouts and a camera roll of screenshots, it can pay for itself fast.
